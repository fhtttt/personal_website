> Should I mark more than shining hours?  
> I have agreed to paint a narrative on the city walls.  
> I have now been at work many years,  
> there is so much to be told.
>
> — Evan S. Connell, Jr., epigraph to Annie Dillard's *For the Time Being*

Almost everyone keeps a record of their life. Photographs, messages, a notes app, a shelf of half-filled journals. Almost nobody treats that record as history. The difference isn't sincerity, and it isn't volume. It's method.

Dillard puts the whole ladder in one sentence, in *An American Childhood*, describing a child coming to know her mother:

> A young child knows Mother as a smelled skin, a halo of light, a strength in the arms, a voice that trembles with feeling. Later the child wakes and discovers this mother — and adds facts to impressions, and historical understanding to facts.

Impressions, then facts, then historical understanding. Most private records stop on the first rung and stay there for a lifetime. The third rung is available to anyone. Reaching it is the most interesting thing you can do with your own past, and it requires nothing but the working habits of historians, which are not difficult, only unfamiliar.

## Already, at the time

The first time the line lodged in me, I was a teenager at my father's funeral.

> 此情可待成追憶，只是當時已惘然。[^jinse]
>
> — Li Shangyin (李商隱, c. 813–858), *Jinse* (錦瑟)

Everyone there took it the way everyone takes it: *this feeling could become something to look back on — it's just that at the time, I didn't realize.* We had it and did not know we had it. Nobody could have known. That is why the line belongs at funerals.

Years later I came to know someone who matters to me more than I know how to write down. She was born on my father's birthday. The same day.

And every second I spend with her, I feel precisely what that line describes. The looking-back. The wistfulness — 惘然.[^wangran] She has not gone anywhere.

That should not be possible. On the standard reading, the feeling is retrospective by definition — it is what arrives *after*, when the thing is out of reach and you finally see what it was. It is not something available in the present tense, with the person across the table, in good health, staying.

So either something was wrong with me, or something was wrong with the reading. I assumed it was me for a long time. That is the more available explanation, and it is the one that keeps you quiet about it.

What finally sent me looking was a second worry stacked on the first: I had taken a line people say over the dead and was using it on someone alive. Chinese has a phrase for that worry — 好污古人, *soiling the ancients*,[^wugu] making the dead say your thing. I had been leaning on a ninth-century poet at the most serious moments of my life and had never once checked whether he was saying what I needed him to say.

He wasn't. It comes down to two words, both of which survive into modern Chinese with different meanings, so a modern reader glides straight past them.

**可待** *kě dài.* In the same poet's *Peonies* (牡丹) the phrase sits in strict parallel with 何曾, a rhetorical *never*: Shi Chong (石崇) was so rich his household never trimmed a candlewick; Xun Yu (荀彧) was so fragrant his censer needed no incense.[^kedai] So 可待 does not mean *can wait to*. It means **why wait**. Notice how that got settled: not by anything before or after it in time, but by what sits beside it.

**只是** *zhǐ shì.* From the Six Dynasties through the Tang, 只 marks scope — *right in, precisely at*. The concessive sense, *only, however*, is a Song development. Test it on a poem every Chinese child memorizes, by Jia Dao (賈島): 只在此山中 is not "only in this mountain" — he is off gathering herbs, of course he is in the mountain — but *right here in this mountain*.[^jiadao] And in this same poet's most famous line, 夕陽無限好，只是近黃昏 is not "beautiful, only it's near dusk" but *beautiful precisely because it is dusk*.[^leyou]

Put both back, and the couplet inverts:

> Why would this feeling wait to become a memory before I knew what it was worth? **Already, at the time**, I was 惘然.

He noticed. He noticed completely, while it was happening. And the word is not wistfulness at all; it is the sensation of a real thing turning unreal in your hands, because you already know you are going to lose it. The loss arrives early and hollows out the present. He has the images for it — *in the palm*, *in the hand*.[^zhangzhong] The pearl is on your palm because it is precious, and because it is on your palm you feel every second of its going.

The consoling reading, it turned out, was not merely inaccurate; it had been doing something to me all along. *You can't know at the time* is an absolution, and an absolution is also a verdict — it says the failure is structural, that every loss still ahead of me will arrive as a surprise I am not equipped for. It buys comfort about the past by taking away your standing in the future.

And it had told me, for years, that what I felt across the table from her was not a thing a person could feel.

Which is to say that nothing was wrong with me. There is a name for what happens when you are with someone you cannot afford to lose and you have already done the arithmetic. A man set it down in the ninth century, precisely, and eleven hundred years of people saying it over graves had been mis-hearing him.

I have never had a stranger experience of being understood.

That correction is what historical understanding does. It did not come from remembering harder. It came from a concordance, two other poems, and one fact about Tang grammar.

## A history of one

I would never have looked up the scope-marking function of 只 out of curiosity. Nobody does; it lives in a footnote read by few people. I looked it up because I needed to know whether I was broken.

No curriculum can issue you that. A syllabus can put the poem in front of you; it cannot make you *need* the answer. This is why so much education stays inert — you take the consensus paraphrase, you pass, and nothing about you has changed. And it is why a private history, done properly, is not a lesser form of scholarship than the public kind. It is the form that has the motive.

What comes out at the end is strange and badly undervalued: **a literary history of one.** A philosophy of one. Not a survey of a field, but a canon assembled entirely from the texts your own life forced you to need, in the order it forced you to need them, each worked to the depth the need demanded. Nobody else's would look anything like it.

The habits that keep it honest are borrowed, and none of them are hard.

**Keep the record apart from what you say about the record.** Sources and interpretation.[^shiliao] What you wrote in 2015 and what you now think 2015 meant are two documents, and the second must never overwrite the first. This one rule prevents most of the damage.

**Don't choose among variants.** Your accounts of the same event at fifteen, twenty-five, and thirty-five are three witnesses, not three drafts. Set them side by side and ask why they diverge. The nicest version isn't the true one; usually it's just the latest.

**Give it a space, not a story.** Andrew Plaks (浦安迪) distinguished Greek myth, organized narratively, from early Chinese myth, which is arranged spatially — fragments in a static field, as in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, where the far countries are laid out by direction and no entry explains any other.[^shanhai] That is the right form for an archive, because narrative smooths: a sequence demands causation, so a life told as a story will manufacture its own connective tissue without your noticing. It is also what makes the previous rule possible. A narrative holds one version of an event; a map holds three, at three positions, indefinitely. A story gives you a reader. A map gives you someone who walks.

**Date and sign every reading.** Then the history of your own changing mind becomes a readable text in its own right — a commentary tradition on yourself.

**Read your past self with your past self's dictionary.** Everyone misreads 只是 by applying a Song gloss to a Tang text and never noticing. You do this to yourself, reading what you wrote at fifteen with the vocabulary of thirty-five. I was worried about soiling the ancients. The ancient I most reliably misread is my own earlier self.

**Leave the unknown unfilled.**[^queyi] A gap honestly marked is worth more than a plausible reconstruction, because within a year you will no longer remember which was which.

## The machine

When I started writing this, I expected to report that a language model would hand you the funeral reading — fluently, in the voice of settled knowledge. So I checked. I put the couplet to several of the current frontier models (July 2026) and they gave me the corrected reading. They did better than the funeral did.

That is worth sitting with, because it dismantles the easy complaint and leaves a harder one.

The right reading was never hard to get. It was in the commentaries. It was taught in a public lecture anyone can watch. Now it comes back in three seconds. And still everyone at my father's funeral said the other thing, and I carried the other thing for years — not because the correct answer was expensive, but because **I never asked**. The consensus reading did not win an argument. It was never in one. It resonated, so it stopped being a question, and a line that is doing its job does not get looked up.

So the machine changes nothing about the actual bottleneck. It has driven the cost of answers to nearly zero, and the cost of answers was never what stopped anyone. What stopped them was having no reason to ask — and that reason has to come from somewhere a machine cannot reach. Mine was the angel with my father's birthday and a feeling that shouldn't have been possible. If anything, cheap answers make the real failure easier: when everything is retrievable, nothing has to be held, and you never sit long enough in the discomfort that would have sent you looking. An abundance of answers is not the presence of questions. Only one of the two is scarce.

Note also that Li Shangyin is the *easy* case. There was a right answer sitting in the record, waiting to be fetched. Your own past has no commentarial tradition, and no scholar is coming to reconstruct it. Ask a model what a period of your life meant and there is nothing to retrieve, so it will do the only thing available to it: return the shape such answers usually take. That is where a fluent consensus is genuinely dangerous, and it is exactly where this method applies.

Which leaves a clean division of labor. Look at what the correct reading actually took: a concordance, two poems by the same man, one fact from the history of grammar. **None of that is beyond a machine** — it is closer to what machines are good at than interpretation is. Ask it for evidence, variants, parallels, contradictions; nobody has ever had a better instrument for that. Bring it the question yourself. Don't ask it what any of it meant. That part is the part that forms you, and it is the only part that stops happening the moment you hand it over.

---

Li Shangyin's affliction was in his hands. He held on, and the holding is what made it unreal. The tighter the grip, the more of the going you feel.

There is a form of love that does not hold. It says: *even if the person beside you is not me, I still love you.* It says: *I will be there when you need me, including after I am gone.* It does not require being present at the moment of its own usefulness. It builds something and steps back, and it leaves quietly when it isn't needed. We already have a word for love in that form. We call it infrastructure, and we don't recognize it as love at all, because whoever built the road is never standing at the roadside when you drive on it.

He could not keep his own life. So he wrote it down — and eleven hundred years later he arrived, on time, for someone who needed him. He built the only system available to him and never knew that it worked.

Connell's speaker has agreed to paint a narrative on the city walls, has been at work for years, and has so much left to tell. But the first line is the real question: *should I mark more than shining hours?* A sundial records only the time the sun is on it. So does an unexamined memory.

We used to be able to assume we would not be read in a thousand years. That is no longer safe. Whatever you leave in the open will be taken in, and something a thousand years from now will be able to retrieve all of it at once.

Which is not the good news it sounds like. Li Shangyin was retrievable the entire time. Being available for eleven hundred years is exactly what he was — and for eleven hundred years he was read wrong, fluently and movingly, by people who loved the line. Retrieval is not understanding. A record that can only be summarized will be summarized, and what comes back will be the center of mass: the version that resonates, which is the version that survives.

What finally broke the consensus was not a better reader. It was another poem. *Peonies* sat in the same collection with 可待 in a position where the funeral meaning is impossible, and one document contradicted the reading of the other. He never annotated *Jinse*. His corpus did it for him, by accident, because more than one thing of his survived.

So keep it, and keep it honestly — not so that you will be found, but so that whoever comes can be corrected by you. Do on purpose what his collection did by accident. Leave the variants in. Date the readings. Mark the gaps. Keep the sources apart from what you said about them. That is the whole difference between an archive that can be paraphrased and an archive that can argue back, and you will not be there to argue.

The first person it has to be able to contradict is you. You are the reader most likely to come, and the one most likely to arrive wanting a flattering answer.

After that, the operation works at a range of a hundred years, and inside a hundred years are people you can name — people who will otherwise be left holding only their memory of you, which will smooth exactly the way yours did. Someone is going to need it. Write as though you will not be there.

The philological argument — 可待 as rhetorical negation, 只是 as Tang-period scope marking, and 惘然 as derealization — is Professor Ou Lijuan's (歐麗娟, National Taiwan University), from her lecture 美麗與哀愁——李商隱的無題 and her research on Tang poetry. So are the reading of 惘然 as a real thing turning unreal in the hand, the 掌中／手中 evidence, her description of the condition as 凌遲, the account of 只 in its Middle Chinese usage, and the citation of Plaks. What appears here is my most charitable summary of her interpretation of the past.

[^jinse]: *Cǐ qíng kě dài chéng zhuīyì, zhǐ shì dāngshí yǐ wǎngrán.* The closing couplet of a poem whose title, 錦瑟, means "the ornamented zither" — among the most quoted lines in the Chinese language. The full poem is the epigraph in the top-left corner of this site's home page.
[^wangran]: 惘然 *wǎngrán*. Usually glossed "wistful, disconsolate," which explains nothing — the reading argued for here is that it names the sensation of a real thing going unreal in your hands.
[^wugu]: 好污古人 — literally "fond of soiling the ancients": the habit of making a dead writer say what you need said, and calling the result consolation.
[^kedai]: 石家蠟燭何曾剪，荀令香爐可待熏。"When would the Shi household ever trim a candlewick; what need has Lord Xun's censer of incense?" Shi Chong (石崇) was a byword for wealth so extreme that candles burned untrimmed day and night; Xun Yu (荀彧), called Lord Xun, was said to leave a room fragrant for three days after sitting in it. Both halves are rhetorical questions meaning *never* — which is what fixes 可待 as 何待, *why wait*.
[^jiadao]: 松下問童子，言師採藥去。只在此山中，雲深不知處。— Jia Dao (賈島), *Seeking the Hermit and Not Finding Him*: "Under the pines I asked the boy. He said his master had gone to gather herbs — right here in this mountain, but the clouds are deep and there is no telling where."
[^leyou]: 樂遊原 *Leyou Heights*, by the same poet: 夕陽無限好，只是近黃昏。
[^zhangzhong]: 掌中 and 手中, *in the palm* and *in the hand*, from the *Yantai* poems (燕臺): 當時歡向掌中銷 — "even then the joy was dissolving in my palm"; 歌唇一世銜雨看，可惜馨香手中故 — "a lifetime of singing with tears in the throat, and the fragrance withers in the closed hand."
[^shiliao]: 史料 and 史論 — the primary sources a history is built from, versus the historian's argument about them. Keeping the two physically apart is the oldest hygiene in the discipline.
[^shanhai]: 山海經, a compilation of the Warring States to early Han period cataloguing the creatures, mountains and far countries of the world. Its entries are arranged by compass direction rather than by any sequence of events, and there are grounds for thinking the text began as annotation to a set of pictures. The imperial *Siku* catalogue declined to classify it as geography at all, calling it instead the oldest work of fiction.
[^queyi]: 闕疑, from the *Analects* (論語·為政): 多聞闕疑，慎言其餘，則寡尤 — "hear much, leave the doubtful parts aside, speak the rest with care, and you will make few mistakes."
